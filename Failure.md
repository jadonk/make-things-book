## A Diagnosis of Failure

Catastrophic failures have often exhibited a common set of fundamental causes. From the Tacoma bridge collapse, the Challenger explosion, the Deepwater Horizon blowout, Boeing's 737 MAX crashes, and the 2003 North American Blackout, to a 50-foot-tall steel molassas storage tank flooding Boston in 1919, the same two driving causes can be observed over and over again: tight coupling and drift.

Tight coupling is when a complex system's proper function depends on elements that are highly connected and interdependent. When failure in one element has a major impact on the rest of the system, you can be confident that trouble is brewing.

Drift is when the common forces of competition, scarcity and production pressure are allowed to gradually increase tolerances beyond an acceptable limit. This is called drift because it often happens slowly in significant disasters, because success can be a false indicator of acceptable risk.

These same causes can be observed in failures with somewhat less obvious harmful effects.
